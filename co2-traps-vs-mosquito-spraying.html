What Is In Mosquito Spray?

Conventional residential mosquito spray programs use synthetic pyrethroid insecticides — most commonly permethrin, bifenthrin, or deltamethrin — applied by truck-mounted or backpack foggers. These are broad-spectrum neurotoxins derived from natural pyrethrins found in chrysanthemum flowers, engineered for extended environmental persistence.

When applied to your yard, these compounds coat foliage, lawn surfaces, soil, and any surfaces the mist contacts. Residual activity lasts from a few hours to several days depending on rain, UV exposure, and product formulation. During this active period, any insect contacting treated surfaces is at risk — mosquitoes, bees, butterflies, fireflies, dragonflies, ground beetles, and aquatic insects in drainage.

A 2019 study published in PLOS ONE found that residential pyrethroid applications reduced bee populations by up to 70% in treated areas, with recovery taking 2-3 weeks after each application cycle.

The Bee Problem

Honeybees, native bees, and bumblebees forage within the same outdoor areas being treated for mosquitoes. Pyrethroids applied to flowering plants — or drifting onto them from neighboring applications — contaminate nectar and pollen that bees carry back to colonies. Sublethal doses disrupt bee navigation and foraging behavior even when they do not cause direct mortality.

In Austin, where native plant gardening is widespread and many homeowners maintain pollinator habitats, conventional spray programs present a direct conflict. Once applied, there is no way to selectively protect bee-frequented plants from pyrethroid coverage.

The Population Rebound Problem

Mosquito spraying kills mosquitoes present at the moment of treatment. It does not address breeding sites, egg deposits, or mosquitoes that were in protected locations (inside vegetation, below the spray zone) during application. Within days to a week, the local population rebounds as new adults emerge from untreated breeding sites.

CO₂ trapping addresses this differently: by continuously removing breeding females before they complete their reproductive cycles, it gradually reduces the total number of mosquitoes being produced locally. The results build over weeks and create sustained suppression rather than cyclical knockdown.

When Spraying Might Still Be Appropriate

There are scenarios where rapid knockdown spraying makes sense: public health emergencies involving West Nile or Zika virus transmission, large-scale event preparation where instant results are required, or severe infestations that need immediate reduction before trapping can suppress the population. In these cases, spraying can be used as a short-term measure alongside a trapping program for long-term management.

For residential use in Austin — where the goal is sustained, family-safe, eco-responsible protection throughout the season — CO₂ trapping is the appropriate primary strategy.

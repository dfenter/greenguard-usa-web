Find out before it stops working, not after.

FleetOps collects what is actually installed and configured at every site and shows it in one list: versions, certificates, backups and anything that has drifted from what you intended.

When something needs changing, you authorize it once and it applies everywhere it should. A certificate rotation across the whole estate becomes one action rather than a week of scheduled visits.

Every instruction is signed, and a site will not accept the same instruction twice, so an action cannot be replayed against your estate by anyone who captures it.

where it earns its keep

Four Situations This Solves

Each one is a job that stays manageable at three sites and stops being manageable at thirty.

The situationWhat changes
Nobody can say what version is running at site 23
The operations lead of an estate that grew one site at a time.		 The inventory lives in a spreadsheet that was accurate the week it was written, so drift, version mismatches and expiring certificates are discovered by something breaking. FleetOps collects what is actually installed and configured at every site and puts it in one list, with the counts by fault class: drift, certificates expiring, certificates already expired, backups overdue, version mismatch. The answer to "what is at site 23" becomes a lookup.
The same change has to be made forty times by hand
Whoever has to schedule forty site visits for one setting.		 Repeating a change forty times is a week of work and forty chances to do it slightly differently, which is where drift comes from in the first place. In FleetOps you authorize the change once and it applies everywhere it should. On a 40-site rig one authorized certificate rotation reached all forty sites in 937 milliseconds, and the sites that were not in scope were left alone.
A site fails, and nobody is sure the backup is current
Anyone who has restored from a backup and found it was six months old.		 Backup age is invisible until the day it matters, and that is the worst day to find out. Backup overdue is a tracked fault class alongside certificates and drift, so an out of date backup shows up on the same screen as everything else, before the failure rather than during it. On the planted-fault rig every fault class surfaced at exactly the expected count.
One console that can change forty sites is itself a risk
The security reviewer who has to sign off on the tool.		 A fleet action captured off the wire and sent again would be a serious problem, so it is closed by design. Every instruction is signed, and a site refuses an instruction it has already accepted. The same rotation instruction, captured and replayed, was refused by all forty sites.

The value of FleetOps grows with site count. At three sites a spreadsheet works. At thirty it does not, and the first expired certificate usually costs more than the software.

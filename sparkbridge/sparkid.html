Know exactly who can touch your plant

Access to a plant network is usually a hand-typed list of patterns. One character in the wrong place is the difference between letting someone read a temperature and letting them operate a pump.

A real finding from our own systems, and the rule that replaced it.

A wildcard should never be something a person types.

You describe intent in plain terms: this site sends its own data, and takes commands from the control system only. SparkID turns that into the exact permissions your broker needs, one line per message type, scoped to that one site.

It gives every system its own credential rather than a shared one, so you can change or revoke access for a single site without taking anything else down. Shared passwords are the reason most plants cannot rotate credentials at all.

Before it changes anything it shows you the difference, and it refuses outright to apply a change that would lock you out of your own broker. That refusal is a feature, not a safety net.

Four Situations This Solves

All four come from the same root: access rules that a person typed and nobody can safely change.

One site is compromised, and it can reach every other site
Any operation running more than one plant on a shared broker.		 A pattern written to cover "the plant" usually covers every site in it, and every message type including the commands that operate equipment. SparkID generates the permissions from stated intent instead, one line per message type, scoped to the single site that owns them. A contractor left six months ago and their credential still works
Anyone with one password shared across several systems.		 Nothing documents what that credential is actually used for, so revoking it is a gamble and it stays live instead. SparkID gives every system its own credential derived from what that system is for, so a single one can be changed or revoked without taking anything else down. Rotation stops being an outage risk, which is the only reason it ever gets done.
